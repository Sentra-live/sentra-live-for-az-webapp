# Is Your Building Ready for Continuous Monitoring? A Pre-Installation Checklist

> A pre-installation checklist for building structural health monitoring in India: purpose, drawings, condition survey, sensor locations, access, signal, alert thresholds and baseline.

Published: 2026-10-27  
Source: https://sentratech.in/blogs/building-continuous-monitoring-readiness-checklist.html

Most delays in a building monitoring project happen before the first sensor goes up: drawings nobody can find, a locked terrace, no signal in the basement, no agreement on who answers an alert. This checklist covers what to have ready before installation day.

Wireless sensors have made the installation itself quick. On most buildings the tiltmeters, crack meters and vibration sensors can be fixed in a day or two. What takes time is everything around them: knowing what the monitoring is for, finding out how the building was built, getting into the rooms where the sensors need to go, and agreeing what happens when a reading crosses a limit. Building owners, housing societies, facility managers and contractors who sort these out in advance get useful data from the first week. Those who do not spend the first month catching up. Here is the checklist we work through with clients before any structural health monitoring installation.

## 1. Write Down Why You Are Monitoring

Every later decision follows from the reason. A building next to a deep basement excavation needs tilt, settlement and vibration on the side facing the dig, read every few minutes while work is under way. An ageing building with visible cracking needs crack meters on the cracks that matter and tilt on the walls they run through, read over at least a full year. A building after a retrofit, a change of use or an added floor needs strain and tilt where the new loads go.

Put the reason in one or two sentences and agree it with everyone involved. "We want to know if the excavation next door is moving our building, and stop the work before it causes damage" leads to a very different system from "we want to know whether the cracks in the east wall are still growing". Both are good reasons. A vague one, such as "we want monitoring", leads to too many sensors in the wrong places.

## 2. Collect the Drawings and Records

The engineer designing the sensor layout needs to know how the building carries its loads. Gather whatever exists:

- **Structural drawings**, ideally as-built: column grid, wall layout, beam and slab details, foundation type and depth.

- **Soil investigation report**, which shows the ground the foundations sit on and the groundwater level.

- **Previous inspection or condition reports**, and any structural audit carried out under local bye-laws.

- **Repair and alteration history**: jacketing, added floors, removed walls, waterproofing work.

- **For adjacent construction**, the neighbouring project's excavation depth, retaining system, dewatering plan and programme.

Many older buildings in India have no surviving drawings. That does not stop monitoring. It means a site survey to confirm the structural system, and it makes the baseline readings more important, because there is less design information to compare against.

## 3. Record the Building's Condition Before Anything Starts

A condition survey before installation is the single most useful document in a monitoring project, and it is often skipped. Walk the building, map every visible crack with its location, length and width, photograph it next to a scale, and note damp patches, spalling, sticking doors and out-of-level floors.

This record does two jobs. It tells the engineer where to put crack meters and tiltmeters. And if neighbouring construction is the reason for monitoring, it is the evidence that settles a later argument about whether a crack was there before the work began. Without it, every crack found later becomes a dispute. Share the survey with the neighbouring contractor and get it acknowledged.

## 4. Agree Where the Sensors Will Go

Sensors go where the risk is. On a typical building the layout draws on a small set of instruments:

- **[Tiltmeters](https://sentratech.in/products/tiltmeter.html)** on columns, shear walls or the facade, often at two or more levels, to show rotation of the structure and of individual walls. Our guide to [inclinometers and digital tiltmeters](https://sentratech.in/blogs/what-is-an-inclinometer-digital-tiltmeter-guide.html) explains how they work.

- **Crack meters** across the active cracks identified in the condition survey.

- **[Vibration meters](https://sentratech.in/products/vibration-meter.html)** on the floor or foundation nearest the source, where piling, demolition, blasting or heavy traffic is the concern.

- **Settlement points** at the plinth, read by precise levelling, where foundation movement is suspected.

- **Temperature** at the sensor locations, so daily and seasonal thermal movement is not mistaken for structural change.

Mark every location on a floor plan and walk it with the person who controls access. A location on a drawing that turns out to be inside a locked electrical room or behind a shop signboard costs a second visit. The parameters themselves are covered in more detail in our article on [what continuous building monitoring measures](https://sentratech.in/blogs/continuous-building-shm-iot-sensors.html).

## 5. Arrange Access and Permissions

Most sensors sit in common areas: basements, stair cores, lift lobbies, the terrace and the facade. Each of those has someone who controls it, and each needs to be in the plan.

- **Residential buildings:** a resolution or written consent from the housing society or residents' association, and notice to residents whose flats are needed.

- **Commercial buildings:** the facility manager's permit-to-work, security passes and any hot-work or working-at-height rules.

- **Facade sensors:** rope access or a boom lift, and in some cities permission for equipment on the pavement.

- **Heritage settings:** if the building is protected, or sits close to a protected monument, check with the competent authority before fixing anything to it. Our article on [monitoring heritage structures](https://sentratech.in/article/heritage-structure-monitoring-india.html) covers why these buildings need a different approach.

## 6. Check Power and Signal at Every Location

Battery-powered wireless sensors remove the need for cable runs and mains power at each location, which is why installation is quick. They still need a radio path to a [gateway](https://sentratech.in/products/gateway.html), and the gateway needs power and a 4G or wired internet connection.

Check this before installation day, not on it. The terrace is usually the best gateway position. Basements, lift pits and rooms surrounded by concrete walls are where signal fails. A quick survey with a test sensor shows which locations reach the gateway and which need a [repeater](https://sentratech.in/products/repeater.html). Agree where the gateway will take power from, and who to call if that socket is switched off.

## 7. Set Thresholds and Decide Who Responds

An alert that reaches nobody, or reaches someone with no authority to act, is worse than no alert. Before monitoring starts, agree three things for each parameter:

1. **Tiered limits:** a watch level that prompts a closer look at the data, an alert level that triggers a site inspection, and an action level that stops the work nearby or restricts use of part of the building.

2. **Named people** for each level, with phone numbers and a deputy, on both the owner's side and the contractor's side if adjacent works are involved.

3. **What each person does** when they receive the alert, written down in advance.

Limits come from the structural engineer, based on the building type, its condition and the relevant standards. The same tiered approach underpins our [six-step early-warning plan](https://sentratech.in/blogs/early-warning-plan-critical-infrastructure-checklist.html) for larger infrastructure.

## 8. Decide Who Sees the Data

A building monitoring project often has several parties with an interest in the data: the owner, the neighbouring developer, their contractor, a structural consultant and sometimes the municipal authority. Agree at the start who gets dashboard access, who receives alerts, how often reports are issued and in what form, and who owns the data at the end of the project. Writing this into the contract avoids an awkward conversation when the readings become important.

## 9. Plan the Baseline Period

The first weeks of readings show how the building normally behaves: how much it tilts between a cool morning and a hot afternoon, how much the cracks open and close, what background vibration looks like. Later readings are judged against this. A reliable baseline typically takes 2 to 6 weeks.

Where monitoring protects a building from adjacent construction, the sensors must go in before excavation, piling or demolition begins. Install them the week work starts and there is no way to show which movement the construction caused. Where the concern is long-term cracking, plan for at least a full year so the seasonal cycle can be separated from real progression.

## 10. Budget for the Whole Duration

The installation is only part of the cost. Plan for the monitoring period itself, data and platform charges, periodic site visits, reporting and removal at the end. The duration usually follows the reason: the length of the neighbouring construction plus a settling period, or a fixed term for a condition assessment. ## Frequently Asked Questions

#### What do I need before installing a building monitoring system?

A clear reason for monitoring, the structural drawings and soil report if they exist, a condition survey with photographs of existing cracks, agreed sensor locations with access to them, a signal check at those locations, alert thresholds with named people to act on them, and agreement on who receives the data.

#### Can a building be monitored if there are no drawings?

Yes. Many older buildings in India have no surviving drawings. The engineer then relies on a site survey to confirm the structural system, and the monitoring baseline becomes the main reference for judging later movement.

#### How long should a building be monitored before nearby construction starts?

Ideally 2 to 6 weeks, so the baseline captures normal daily and thermal movement before excavation, piling or demolition begins. Without that baseline it is hard to show which movement the construction caused.

#### Do residents have to move out while sensors are installed?

No. Wireless sensors are small, battery powered and fixed with a few anchors or adhesive, usually in common areas, basements, terraces and on the facade. Access to individual flats is needed only where a sensor has to go inside one.
