## Executive Summary

Highway bridges are some of the most valuable assets in a transport network, yet most are still managed through periodic inspection, which says how a bridge looked on the day of the visit and not much more. As traffic grows and bridges age, owners need to know how each one behaves under the loads it actually carries.

Structural health monitoring (SHM) uses field sensors, automatic data collection and analytics to report a structure's behaviour all the time. Engineers see small changes when they start, without waiting for the next inspection, and can plan maintenance from measured performance.

This case study describes an SHM system Sentra installed on a major highway bridge, what the first year of data showed, and how it helped the owner move from reactive to condition-based maintenance.

## Project Background

The bridge is a four-lane reinforced concrete structure that has carried a major transport corridor for more than thirty years. About 55,000 vehicles cross it every day, many of them heavy freight trucks, so it carries constant dynamic loading well beyond what the original design expected.

Routine inspections had always rated the bridge structurally sound, with minor weathering and some local surface deterioration. But each inspection only captured one day. None of them showed how the structure responded between visits as traffic, weather and the materials themselves changed.

The owner wanted continuous monitoring to fill those gaps: a reliable performance baseline, better long-term visibility of the structure, and maintenance planning based on measured behaviour instead of assumptions.

## The problem with monitoring ageing highway bridges

Visual inspection finds what can be seen, such as cracks, corrosion and surface damage. It can't measure how the structure responds to load, and it misses gradual changes that only become clear as a trend across many readings.

Several things made continuous monitoring more pressing:

- More freight and heavier axle loads add millions of loading cycles to the bridge each year.

- Seasonal temperature changes, rain, humidity and water ingress all speed up concrete deterioration, reinforcement corrosion and fatigue.

- After thirty-plus years in service, creep, shrinkage and fatigue start to change how the structure behaves.

- Fixed inspection schedules push owners toward cautious guesses. Without much performance data, they either step in when nothing was needed or wait too long.

The owner needed continuous structural data, early warning of deterioration, and a long enough record to plan maintenance over the bridge’s remaining life.

## Project objectives

The aim was continuous visibility of how the bridge performs. Specifically, to:

- Establish continuous monitoring of critical structural components

- Measure structural response under operational traffic loads

- Capture performance data between routine inspection cycles

- Detect early indicators of infrastructure deterioration

- Support condition-based and predictive maintenance

- Give engineers real-time structural data to base decisions on

- Keep the bridge in service longer without compromising safety or reliability

## Structural Health Monitoring Solution Overview

Sentra installed an integrated [Structural Health Monitoring](https://sentratech.in/solutions/structural-health-monitoring.html) system, with field sensors, automatic data acquisition, cloud connectivity and engineering analytics on one platform.

The system measures how the bridge responds to traffic and weather around the clock. Readings are collected, processed and sent to a central dashboard, so engineers can check the bridge's performance in near real time.

Comparing current behaviour with the baseline tells engineers whether a movement is normal or abnormal. Alert thresholds sit on top of that comparison. When a monitored value crosses a set engineering limit, someone is notified and can investigate while the problem is still small.

The same data also feeds maintenance planning, lifecycle forecasts, rehabilitation priorities and any future engineering assessment of the bridge.

## Implementation Phases

Installation was done in four phases, planned to get accurate data while disrupting traffic as little as possible.

#### Phase 1: Structural Assessment

Sentra's engineers went through the design drawings, past inspection reports, traffic data, environmental conditions and maintenance records to decide which components needed continuous monitoring. That review set where the sensors would go and how the system would be configured.

#### Phase 2: Sensor Deployment

Sensors were fitted to the main girders, piers, bearings, expansion joints and the deck sections carrying the heaviest loads. Each was calibrated and checked on site so it would stay accurate in field conditions.

#### Phase 3: System Integration

Data loggers and secure communication links connect the sensors to the central platform. Dashboards, automatic alerts and performance thresholds were set up from the design criteria and the expected structural behaviour.

#### Phase 4: Continuous Monitoring

Since commissioning, monitoring has run 24/7. Engineers can see how the bridge is performing at any time, whatever the traffic or weather, and the same setup can be extended to other structures as the owner adds them.

## Results from Continuous Structural Monitoring

The first year gave a complete dataset on how the bridge behaves in real operation, covering daily traffic cycles and every season, instead of a handful of separate inspection visits.

#### Traffic Load Performance

The bridge responded predictably to normal traffic and stayed within its design limits. The data did show that some girders consistently carry higher strain during peak freight hours. Those values are within acceptable engineering limits, but they are the clearest evidence of what growing commercial traffic is doing to the structure, and they now feed directly into maintenance planning.

#### Environmental Behaviour

Structural movement tracked the weather closely. Temperature swings caused the expected thermal expansion and contraction, and long periods of rain changed how the bearings and supports moved. Looking at structural and environmental data together lets engineers tell normal seasonal behaviour from a real problem.

#### Long-Term Performance Trends

Overall, structural performance was stable. Some components showed gradual changes in strain and displacement. None of it is an immediate safety risk, but it is an early sign of deterioration, and those components remain under close watch.

## Engineering Evaluation of Bridge Performance

Above all, continuous monitoring replaces assumptions with evidence.

With continuous performance data, not just periodic inspection reports, engineers could:

- Evaluate actual structural behaviour under operational loading

- Compare measured performance against original design assumptions

- Assess the impact of increasing freight traffic on critical structural elements

- Quantify the effects of temperature and environmental conditions

- Identify gradual performance changes before visible deterioration occurred

Maintenance is now prioritised by measured condition instead of the inspection calendar. Money and effort go to the components that are actually changing, and work that was never needed gets dropped.

The historical record the system has built up is now the reference for future inspections, rehabilitation projects and lifecycle assessments.

## What changed for the owner

The system changed how the owner manages the bridge in several practical ways.

- Small structural changes are found before they turn into visible defects or expensive repairs.

- Maintenance planning, rehabilitation priorities and lifecycle management are now based on measured structural data as well as engineering judgement. - Work is scheduled by the bridge's actual condition rather than a fixed inspection interval. Unneeded maintenance is cut and reliability improves.

- Budget and engineering time go where the measurements show they are needed.

- Automatic alerts start an investigation as soon as a monitored value crosses its threshold, and the bridge stays open while engineers look into it.

- Acting early lowers the long-term risk of deterioration, so the bridge lasts longer and the original investment goes further.

## Conclusion

Periodic inspection alone isn't enough to manage a bridge. Traffic, weather and ageing materials all act on the structure between inspections and change how it performs.

Continuous monitoring covers that gap. Sensors and analytics help owners catch deterioration sooner, plan maintenance better and keep lifecycle costs in check.
