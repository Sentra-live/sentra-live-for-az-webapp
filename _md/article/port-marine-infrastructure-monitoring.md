# Port & Marine Infrastructure Monitoring: Protecting Jetties, Quays & Offshore Structures

> How port infrastructure monitoring protects jetties, quay walls and offshore structures: corrosion, tilt, settlement, berthing and mooring loads, sensors, architecture and implementation.

Published: 2026-09-25  
Source: https://sentratech.in/article/port-marine-infrastructure-monitoring.html

Jetties, quay walls and offshore structures sit in one of the harshest environments any asset owner manages. Salt, tide, waves and berthing ships wear them down continuously, yet most are still inspected only a few times a year. Port infrastructure monitoring closes that gap with sensors that report condition every day, not every survey cycle.

A port runs on a small number of structures that cannot fail: the jetty a tanker berths against, the quay wall that carries container cranes, the breakwater that makes the harbour usable at all. When one of them is taken out of service, the cost is measured not only in repairs but in lost berth days, diverted vessels and contractual penalties.

These structures are also some of the hardest to inspect. The damage that matters most tends to be below the waterline, inside the concrete, or in the soil behind the wall. A diver survey or a visual walkdown every year or two shows what is visible on the day. It says little about how fast things are changing, or what happened during the last storm.

This article explains what port infrastructure monitoring measures, which sensors are used on jetties, quays and offshore structures, how a monitoring system is put together, and how asset owners can plan a programme that turns data into maintenance decisions.

## What Is Port Infrastructure Monitoring?

Port infrastructure monitoring is the continuous measurement of how marine structures behave and deteriorate over time. Sensors permanently installed on jetties, wharves, quay walls, dolphins, breakwaters and offshore platforms record parameters such as corrosion activity, tilt, settlement, strain, vibration, water pressure and mooring loads, and send those readings to a central platform.

The purpose is not to replace inspection. It is to fill the long gaps between inspections with measured data, so that engineers can see trends, correlate them with tides, storms and vessel traffic, and act while a problem is still cheap to fix. In the wider field of [structural health monitoring](https://sentratech.in/solutions/structural-health-monitoring.html), marine structure monitoring is one of the most demanding applications, because the environment attacks both the structure and the instruments.

## Why Marine Structures Deteriorate Faster

A reinforced concrete deck on land and the same deck over seawater may be designed to similar codes, but they age very differently. Four mechanisms drive most deterioration in ports.

##### 1. Chloride-induced corrosion

Seawater chlorides migrate through concrete cover until they reach the reinforcement and break down its protective layer. Once corrosion starts, the expanding rust cracks and spalls the concrete, which lets chlorides in faster. Steel piles and sheet piles corrode directly, losing section thickness year by year.

##### 2. Cyclic and impact loading

Every berthing is an impact load on fenders and the structure behind them. Waves, mooring line surges and crane operations add millions of load cycles over a structure's life. That is a fatigue problem for steel and a cracking problem for concrete.

##### 3. Scour and loss of support

Currents, wave action and propeller wash from ever larger vessels erode seabed material around piles and at the toe of quay walls. Loss of embedment reduces lateral capacity and can trigger wall rotation or settlement.

##### 4. Changing water pressure and ground movement

Tides change water pressure behind quay walls twice a day. If drainage clogs, the difference between water levels in front of and behind the wall grows, adding load the wall was never designed to carry. Reclaimed land behind many quays also continues to consolidate for years.

##### The Splash Zone Problem

The most aggressive corrosion usually happens in the splash and tidal zones, where steel and concrete are repeatedly wetted and dried. It is also the zone that is hardest to reach for inspection. That combination is exactly why continuous monitoring earns its place on marine structures.

## What to Monitor on Jetties, Quays and Offshore Structures

Each structure type has its own dominant risks, so a monitoring plan starts with the asset, not the sensor catalogue.

## Jetty Corrosion Monitoring

Corrosion is the slowest failure in a port and the most expensive to ignore. By the time rust staining and spalling are visible from a boat, the reinforcement has usually been corroding for years. Jetty corrosion monitoring aims to see it coming.

**Embedded corrosion sensors.** Installed in the concrete cover at different depths, these show how far the chloride front has advanced towards the reinforcement. The output answers the question owners actually need answered: how many years until corrosion starts at the steel, and where.

**Cathodic protection monitoring.** Many steel piles and sheet piles are protected by sacrificial anodes or impressed current systems. Permanent reference electrodes confirm the steel is still held at a protective potential. A drift out of range is an early sign that anodes are consumed or the system has failed.

**Condition context.** Corrosion data means more when it sits next to temperature, humidity and strain data, plus the results of periodic thickness gauging and half-cell surveys. The monitoring platform becomes the place where all of that is kept together, instead of in separate inspection reports.

Inspection tells you the condition of a jetty on the day of the survey. Monitoring tells you the rate of change, and the rate of change is what sets the maintenance budget.

## Quay Wall Sensors: Tilt, Settlement and Water Pressure

Quay walls rarely fail suddenly without warning. They rotate, settle and bulge first, usually in response to something measurable: dredging in front of the wall, heavier cranes or cargo on the apron, blocked drainage, or scour at the toe. Quay wall sensors are chosen to capture both the movement and its cause.

- **Tiltmeters** on the wall face and capping beam detect rotation long before it can be seen. Sentra's [wireless tiltmeters](https://sentratech.in/products/tiltmeter.html) suit long walls where running cables is impractical.

- **GNSS and survey prisms** give absolute displacement and settlement of the wall and apron. A [GNSS meter](https://sentratech.in/products/gnss-meter.html) provides a continuous three-dimensional record at key points.

- **Piezometers** in the backfill show whether water pressure behind the wall is following the tide as designed or building up because drainage has failed.

- **Strain gauges** on piles, anchors and tie rods show how load is being shared. [Strain gauges](https://sentratech.in/products/strain-gauges.html) are the most direct way to check a structure against its design assumptions.

- **Inclinometers** in the retained soil reveal deep-seated ground movement that surface sensors would miss.

The value comes from reading these together. Wall rotation that follows the tide and recovers is normal behaviour. Rotation that ratchets a little further after each spring tide or each dredging campaign is a trend that needs an engineer. The same principles apply to broader [geotechnical and foundation monitoring](https://sentratech.in/solutions/geotechnical-and-foundation-monitoring.html).

## Berthing, Mooring and Dynamic Loads

Vessels have grown faster than many of the berths that serve them. A jetty designed decades ago may now routinely handle ships well beyond its original design vessel, and nobody knows the actual loads unless they are measured.

**Mooring loads** are measured with load pins or load cells in quick-release hooks and bollards. Real-time line tension helps terminal operators react to surging vessels and gives engineers a record of the peak loads the structure has actually carried.

**Berthing loads** are inferred from fender compression, strain in the berthing structure and approach speed data from docking aid systems. Recording every berthing builds a load history that can be compared against the design berthing energy.

**Dynamic response** from waves, cranes and berthing impacts is captured by [accelerometers](https://sentratech.in/products/accelerometers.html). Changes in natural frequency over time can point to loss of stiffness, damaged connections or loss of foundation support.

## Offshore Structures and Scour

Offshore terminals, single point moorings, platforms and exposed jetty heads face the same mechanisms as nearshore structures, with less access and higher consequences. Fatigue at welded joints, wave-induced vibration and foundation scour dominate the risk profile.

Monitoring here tends to focus on a small number of high-value measurements: strain at critical joints, acceleration to track dynamic response, tilt of the structure, and corrosion protection status. Scour is usually tracked with periodic bathymetric surveys, with structural sensors providing the continuous signal between surveys. Low-power wireless links and solar power reduce the number of cables and the maintenance visits a remote structure needs.

## How a Port Monitoring System Is Built

A reliable marine structure monitoring system is layered, and each layer has to be designed for salt, water and long service life.

1. Sensors Corrosion, tilt, strain, displacement, pressure, load and vibration sensors in IP68, corrosion-resistant housings

2. Data Acquisition Wireless data loggers and nodes mounted above the splash zone, powered by battery or solar

3. Communications Long-range, low-power radio to a gateway, then cellular or fibre to the cloud or the port network

4. Platform & Analytics Data storage, trend analysis, alarm thresholds and correlation with tide, weather and vessel records

5. Decisions Dashboards, alerts and reports that feed maintenance planning, operating limits and capital planning

Two design choices matter more in ports than anywhere else. First, keep electronics out of the splash zone: sensors can be low on the structure, but loggers, radios and power should sit where they can be reached and stay dry. Second, plan for the monitoring life: an instrument that needs a diver to replace it should be specified for the full duration of the programme.

## Challenges and Practical Considerations

- **Harsh environment**: Salt spray, submersion, UV and marine growth attack sensors, cables and brackets. Address it with IP68 enclosures, stainless or coated fixings, protected cable routes and electronics kept above the splash zone.

- **Access and installation windows**: Work on berths competes with vessel schedules and tides, and underwater installation needs divers. Address it by planning installation around berth downtime and favouring wireless sensors that need less cabling.

- **Separating signal from tide**: Many readings move with the tide and temperature every day. Address it by recording tide and weather alongside structural data and setting thresholds on trends, not single readings.

- **Power and communications**: Jetty heads and offshore structures may be far from power and network. Address it with low-power loggers, solar power sized for the monsoon, and long-range radio.

- **Data without ownership**: Alarms nobody is responsible for get ignored. Address it by agreeing thresholds, escalation and response actions before the system goes live.

## How to Plan a Port Monitoring Programme

- **Rank the structures** by consequence of failure and known condition, and start with the berths where an outage would hurt most.

- **Define the questions**: remaining corrosion life, safe vessel size, wall stability after dredging, or life extension of an ageing jetty.

- **Choose parameters and sensors** that answer those questions, and no more.

- **Capture a baseline**: an accurate as-built survey or scan plus an initial period of readings in normal operation.

- **Set thresholds and responses** with the structural engineer, the harbour master and the maintenance team.

- **Integrate the data** with inspection records, tide and vessel data so readings can be interpreted in context.

- **Review and expand**: use the first year of data to refine thresholds and decide which structures to add next.
